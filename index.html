ALS Post-Mortem RNA-seq  ·  Cell-Type Deconvolution  ·  WGCNA

Decoding ALS
at the Cellular Level

Weighted Gene Co-Expression Network Analysis across three CNS regions, with cell-type deconvolution to isolate neuron-specific transcriptional signatures.

Explore the Analysis View Full Reports ↓
Scroll

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) destroys motor neurons across the brain and spinal cord. But bulk RNA-seq blurs cell types together—making it impossible to know whether a gene change happens inside a neuron or in the surrounding glia. This analysis uses cell-type deconvolution to separate neuronal signals from noise, then applies WGCNA to find coordinated gene modules that rise or fall together with ALS severity— independently of RNA quality.

Three CNS regions were analysed: Frontal Cortex, Motor Cortex, and Spinal Cord. New trait columns—patient age, FTD/Alzheimer's comorbidity, and motor onset site (Limb, Bulbar, Axial)—were added to the WGCNA trait matrix to map ALS-associated modules against clinical and demographic context. Modules were retained only if they correlated with ALS (p < 0.05) but not with RNA integrity (p > 0.05), ensuring findings reflect biology, not sample quality.

Pipeline

Three-Step Approach

Each brain region went through the same reproducible pipeline, tuned for deconvoluted floating-point expression data rather than raw integer read counts.

Step 01
Cell-Type Deconvolution
Bulk RNA-seq was computationally decomposed into cell-type-specific expression estimates. For the Frontal Cortex, excitatory and inhibitory neurons were analysed separately using log₂(x+1) normalisation — required because deconvoluted values are floating-point, not integer read counts.
WGCNA-ready prep
Step 02
WGCNA Network Analysis
Weighted Gene Co-Expression Networks were built per region and cell type. Parameters were optimised: minModuleSize=30, mergeCutHeight=0.10, deepSplit=3. Modules were correlated with ALS status, RIN, sex, age, C9orf72, FTD/AD comorbidity, and motor onset site.
Soft thresholding · TOM · ME correlations
Step 03
GO Term Enrichment
Significant modules were taken through Gene Ontology enrichment (clusterProfiler, org.Hs.eg.db) across all three ontologies — Biological Process, Molecular Function, and Cellular Component — using the expressed gene universe as background.
BP · MF · CC  |  BH correction
Frontal Cortex

11 modules, all trending
down in ALS.

The frontal cortex showed coordinated, unidirectional downregulation in ALS — all 11 modules that passed the quality filter had negative correlations with ALS status. The strongest signal was a module with r = −0.346 (p = 0.000145), suggesting broad suppression of frontal cortex transcriptional programmes in disease.

Cell-type deconvolution revealed the source: the signal originates specifically from excitatory neurons. A focused 206-gene excitatory module (r = −0.264, p = 0.000444) was the key deconvoluted finding. Inhibitory neurons showed no significant ALS-associated modules — consistent with excitatory projection neurons being the primary ALS target in the frontal cortex.

Motor Cortex

11 modules, pulling
in both directions.

The motor cortex tells a richer story: 11 modules passed the filter, but they split in direction — 6 downregulated and 5 upregulated in ALS. This bidirectionality is biologically meaningful. Downregulated modules likely reflect motor neuron loss and silencing of neuronal gene programmes. Upregulated modules point toward reactive processes — microglial activation, astrogliosis, and inflammatory gene induction that ramp up as neurons are lost.

The top module showed r = −0.196 (p = 0.000421), with the most significant upregulated module at r = +0.153 (p = 0.00597). The divergent signals suggest that bulk motor cortex RNA-seq captures both the loss of neurons and the gain of reactive cell populations simultaneously.

Spinal Cord

The strongest
ALS signal — by far.

The spinal cord is the primary site of lower motor neuron degeneration in ALS, and the data reflects this clearly. Nine modules passed the filter, with statistical power orders of magnitude stronger than the cortical regions. The top module reached r = −0.357 with p = 1.05 × 10⁻¹⁴ — vanishingly small, indicating a rock-solid association with ALS status.

As with the motor cortex, the spinal cord shows bidirectional changes: 4 modules are downregulated (reflecting motor neuron loss and synaptic silencing) and 5 are upregulated (inflammatory and glial reactive programmes). The magnitude and significance of the spinal cord modules far exceeds those of either cortical region, consistent with the spinal cord being the epicentre of ALS pathology.

Deconvolution Highlight

Isolating the Neuronal Signal

Bulk transcriptomics mixes neuronal, glial, and immune cell signals. Cell-type deconvolution strips that away — and reveals a finding invisible in the bulk data.

Frontal Cortex · Excitatory Neurons · Brown Module

A 206-gene excitatory neuron module is significantly lost in ALS.

After deconvoluting the frontal cortex bulk RNA-seq into excitatory and inhibitory neurons separately, a compact 206-gene module in excitatory neurons emerged as the single most significant ALS-associated finding. It is strongly and significantly downregulated (r = −0.264, p = 0.000444), and was stable across every parameter combination tested — confirming it is a robust biological signal, not a tuning artefact. This module likely captures the loss of a specific excitatory neuron transcriptional programme that is selectively vulnerable in ALS.

Inhibitory neurons, by contrast, showed no significant ALS modules across any parameter setting. The WGCNA scale-free topology fit (R²) never exceeded 0.22 for inhibitory neuron data — indicating GABAergic interneurons do not undergo coordinated co-expression changes in ALS frontal cortex, consistent with their selective sparing relative to excitatory projection neurons.

Cell-Type Resolution

Excitatory vs. Inhibitory Neurons

Frontal cortex deconvolution split neurons into two classes. Their ALS profiles could not be more different.

⚡
Excitatory Neurons
Glutamatergic excitatory neurons produced a well-structured WGCNA network (R² reached the default threshold at soft power = 12). One module — 206 genes, strongly DOWN in ALS — was stable across all parameter combinations including deepSplit=3, mergeCutHeight=0.10, and minModuleSize=30. GO enrichment of this module reveals the specific biological functions being lost inside excitatory neurons in ALS frontal cortex.
✓ Significant · p = 0.000444
🔇
Inhibitory Neurons
GABAergic inhibitory interneurons produced a poorly differentiated network regardless of parameter tuning. The WGCNA scale-free fit R² never exceeded 0.22 across any tested soft power (1–20), meaning the data violates the scale-free topology assumption. No ALS-significant modules emerged. This is most likely a true biological finding: inhibitory interneurons are relatively spared in ALS, consistent with TDP-43 and FUS pathology preferentially targeting excitatory projection neurons.
✗ No significant modules · R² < 0.22

Methodology

Quality-Controlled Filtering

Modules were retained only after passing a two-criterion independence test, and new clinical variables were added to enrich the trait correlation matrix.

🎯
ALS Significance
Each module's eigengene must correlate with ALS status at p < 0.05. This ensures only modules with genuine ALS-associated expression patterns are retained for analysis.
🧪
RIN Independence
The same module's correlation with RNA Integrity Number (RIN) must be non-significant (p > 0.05). This rules out artefacts where gene changes track sample degradation rather than disease biology.
🗂
New Clinical Traits
Five new columns were added: patient Age; FTD/AD comorbidity (binary 0/1 from Subject Group Subcategory); and one-hot encoded motor onset site — Limb, Bulbar, and Axial (generalised onset counted in all three).
⚙️
WGCNA Parameters
Network construction used minModuleSize=30 (professor recommendation), mergeCutHeight=0.10 to prevent over-merging, and deepSplit=3 for more granular module detection. Soft power was auto-selected or manually overridden when the slope was still positive.
